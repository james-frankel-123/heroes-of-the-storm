Replay Intelligence

A companion to the draft coach that works on the other end of the match: parse the .StormReplay files already sitting on a player's disk and explain what actually happened — where the fights were, who was out of position, where the map went quiet, and which decisions moved the win probability.

0 Why this, and why now

The draft coach answers a question that lasts ninety seconds. This answers the one players actually argue about afterwards.

Every Storm League game a player finishes writes a full replay to their own disk. On the development machine there are already 3,379 of them — a private, per-player corpus that needs no API, no scraping, no rate limit and no permission. The draft engine already knows what a good composition looks like; nothing in the product yet knows what a good game looks like.

Framing decision
This is an explanation tool, not a scoring tool. "You were 3rd in hero damage" is a stat sheet, and players already have one. "Your team lost the 14:20 fight because two of you were still walking back from the top camp" is an explanation. Everything below is chosen to support the second kind of sentence.

The one number that shapes this whole product: the parser hands you a position for every hero every second, but roughly 93% of those are interpolated. Real recorded fixes land a median of 15 seconds apart. Any feature that needs to know where someone stood at a particular instant is building on an estimate, and must say so.

Reconstructing the gap

A 15-second position anchor is coarse, but the command stream is not. Between two anchors there are typically dozens of CCmdUpdateTargetPointEvent destinations, and heroes move at known speeds. Paths can therefore be reconstructed to far better than 15s fidelity — as an inference, clearly labelled, never as a recorded fact. The camera stream is a second, independent trace: it says where a player was looking, which is often the more interesting question.

2 Fidelity budget — what we can and can't honestly claim

Deciding this up front is what stops the tool making confident claims it cannot support. Each question is rated by how well the recorded data backs it.

QuestionSupportBasis
Where did people die, and to whom? Exact. Time, coordinates and full kill attribution are recorded.
When were the teamfights? Death clustering in time and space; deaths are exact.
Who rotated, and when? A rotation takes 20–40s, comfortably above the 15s anchor rate.
Who soaked, who wasted XP? Lane presence from anchors, cross-checked against the per-minute XP curve.
Was the player watching the fight before it started? Camera stream is dense; a genuinely novel angle no HotS tool surfaces well.
Was that a good engage? Inferable from positions + cooldowns + outcome, but a judgement, not a measurement.
Did you dodge that skillshot? Was your spacing right? Out of scope. Needs sub-second true positions the replay does not record.
Scope decision
Macro only. The tool analyses rotations, soak, fight timing, objective contests and map attention. It will not claim to analyse mechanical micro. This is a data limit, not a temporary one — no amount of engineering recovers positions the replay never wrote.

3 The pieces

Piece 1 · Replay ingest & the event spine

One pass over a .StormReplay that produces a normalised timeline: hero life-segments stitched per player (the parser emits one unit instance per life, so Murky alone produced eleven), positions tagged recorded-vs-interpolated, deaths, objectives, XP and the command stream reduced to what later stages need. Everything downstream reads this spine, never the raw parser.

Piece 2 · Teamfight detection

The keystone. Cluster hero deaths in time and space, then widen each cluster backwards to the commitment point using command density and converging movement. Output: a list of fights with participants, duration, location, and a result. Almost every later feature is phrased in terms of "the fight at 14:20", so this ships before anything cosmetic.

Piece 3 · Map timeline & heatmaps

Death and kill heatmaps per team and per player; lane-presence bands over time; a scrubbable map view. Coordinates are already normalised — the probe game reports a 248 × 208 grid alongside the map name, so overlaying game space on a map image is a fixed transform per battleground.

Piece 4 · Attention & awareness

Built on the camera stream. Where was each player looking in the ten seconds before a fight started? How much of the game did they spend with the camera on their own hero versus elsewhere? This is the piece with no real equivalent in existing HotS tooling, and it is cheap once Piece 2 defines the fights.

Piece 5 · Draft engine tie-in

The one thing this product can do that a generic replay site cannot: run the existing on-device model over the actual draft, then compare its projected win probability against what happened. "The model gave you 54% out of draft; you were behind on XP from 6:00" turns two separate products into one story.

Piece 6 · Where it lives

Deliberately undecided. Three plausible homes: a tab in the existing overlay app (parsing already happens there), a local report the app writes after a match, or a web view backed by the ingest endpoint from the draft companion's M3. The first is cheapest and needs no backend; the third is the only one that lets a player share a game. Decide after Piece 2, when the shape of the output is known.

Milestones

Same rule as the draft companion: sequenced by dependency, gated on done and verified, no calendar estimates. Each one ends on something you can look at.

A0 is already done — the data question was settled by measurement before any of this was planned, using a throwaway probe now kept in tools/replay-probe. Everything from A1 on is unstarted.

A2
Teamfight detection. Fights identified with participants, timing and outcome. Gate: a human watching the replay agrees with the fight list. If detection is unreliable, everything above it is unreliable, so this is the real go/no-go.

Map timeline & heatmaps. The first thing a player would actually open — deaths, presence, and a scrubbable map.
A4

A5
Draft tie-in & batch mode. Model projection versus outcome, then run it over the whole local replay folder for trends across hundreds of games rather than one.

Deliberately not on this roadmap

  • Live in-match coaching. Replays only exist after the game; anything live is a different product with a different risk profile.
  • Mechanical / micro feedback. Ruled out by the fidelity budget above, not by effort.
  • Other players' replays. The corpus is what the player has on disk. Anything wider needs a source and a conversation about consent.

Risks & open questions

Patch fragility. The replay format shifts per game build — the same standing maintenance tail the battlelobby reader already carries. A parser bump can silently change field meanings rather than failing loudly, so ingest needs assertions that shout when a replay parses but yields nonsense.
Interpolation honesty. The single biggest way this product could mislead is by drawing a smooth confident path between two fixes 30 seconds apart. Interpolated segments must be visually distinct from recorded ones, everywhere they appear.
Teamfight detection is a judgement call. Clustering deaths finds skirmishes with kills, but a fight that ends with everyone retreating leaves no deaths and no obvious signature. Expect the first pass to miss those, and decide whether missing them matters.
Not an anti-cheat concern. This reads files the game has already written, after the match, on the player's own machine. It touches no running process — a strictly weaker footprint than the overlay, which is itself passive.

Open questions

  • Does the camera stream cover all ten players, or only the replay's own observer perspective? Determines whether Piece 4 is a personal tool or a team one.
  • Is TeamObjectives rich enough per battleground? The probe game reported only 2 entries for a full Volskaya match, which looks thin.
  • Do the 3,379 local replays span enough patches to support trend analysis, or does format drift fragment the corpus?
